Be Indiscrete: The Benefits of Learning Continuous Spine Degeneration Severity Scores

Abstract

Lumbar spine degeneration is a major contributor to chronic low back pain and is routinely assessed on MRI using ordinal grading systems, e.g. normal, mild, moderate, severe. Consequently, most approaches to train models to grade these MRIs formulate grading as a multi-class classification problem, treating ordinal grades as categorical, ignoring differences in misclassification severity, and imposing hard decision boundaries on a continuous disease process. This work explores modeling spinal degeneration as a continuous severity ranking problem. We introduce SpineRankNet, a framework that learns scalar severity scores from lumbar spinal MRI, and compare it against multi-class classification and ordinal regression. Using multiple degeneration measures from the Genodisc dataset, we show that a model trained using a ranking loss to produce a continuous score enables fine-grained ordering of MRI scans. Furthermore, the ordinal grading classes can be recovered from the score with comparable accuracy to those from a model trained directly for classification. The score learned by ranking even improves discrimination between more distant classes. Source code is available at https://github.com/spinetools/spineranknet.

Method

SpineRankNet is trained in two phases. First, a shared 3D ResNet-18 encoder (asymmetric strides that preserve the slice dimension) and one linear classification head per grading task are trained from scratch with inverse-frequency-weighted cross-entropy. Second, a lightweight two-layer MLP ranking head per task is trained on top of the classification heads (Fig. 1), while the encoder is fine-tuned at a lower learning rate, using pairs of discs drawn from each mini-batch; the classification heads are also updated, by the ranking loss only. Pairs with different grades are penalised by a severity-weighted squared hinge loss whose adaptive margin m = mbase + mscale · w/S grows with the normalised grade gap w, and pairs with the same grade by a similarity term (their squared score difference) that keeps each grade compact on the severity axis. Each score is soft-clamped to [0, S = 10], and the ordinal grades are recovered from it with per-task thresholds found by coordinate-descent grid search on the validation set.

Severity-weighted hinge loss over pairs (xi, xj) with different grades for task t, where rijt = sign(yit − yjt) and Δsijt = sit − sjt:

ℒhinget = 1|𝒫t| ∑(xi,xj)∈𝒫t wijt [max(0,mijt−rijtΔsijt)] 2

Difference-specific weight and severity-aware margin (Kt grades for task t, S = 10, mbase = 0.5, mscale = 1.5):

wijt = |yit−yjt| ⋅ SKt−1 mijt = mbase + mscale ⋅ wijtS

Similarity term over same-grade pairs, and total loss over the T tasks (λdis = 1, λsim = 0.25):

ℒsimt = 1|𝒮t| ∑(xi,xj)∈𝒮t (Δsijt)2 ℒ = ∑t=1T (λdisℒhinget+λsimℒsimt)

Bounded score: the ranking head φt gives a latent score ht, which a softplus soft-clamp (sp(z) = ln(1 + ez), β = 5) maps to [0, S]:

st = S ⋅ sp(βht) sp(βht)+sp(β(S−ht))

Training pairs come from mini-batches of B = 32 discs: per task, 70% of the candidate pairs are selected by largest violation (hard-negative mining) and 30% are sampled uniformly. With B = 32 and at most 500 pairs per task, all 496 in-batch pairs are used; the 70/30 selection only applies when there are more than 500 candidate pairs.

Dataset

Experiments use the multi-centre Genodisc dataset: T1- and T2-weighted sagittal lumbar MRIs of about 2,000 subjects from five European centres with heterogeneous acquisition parameters (0.2–3.0 T, 0.25–0.9 mm in-plane, 2.6–6.0 mm slice spacing). Each subject has up to six lumbar intervertebral disc (IVD) levels (T12-L1 to L5-S1), and expert radiological annotations cover 11 grading tasks per level: Pfirrmann grade, disc narrowing, central canal stenosis, spondylolisthesis, upper and lower endplate defects, left and right foraminal stenosis, herniation, and anterior and posterior bulging. IVD volumes of 128 × 256 × 12 voxels are extracted with SpineNetV2 vertebra localisation, disc-centred rotation and resampling, and the data is split at patient level into training, validation and test sets (80/10/10%), stratified by acquisition centre and grade distribution. The labels are highly imbalanced: for 10 of the 11 tasks, 60–96% of discs are graded normal (Pfirrmann, whose lowest grade I covers 32% of discs, is the exception), and the most severe grades are rare (e.g. severe stenosis: 2%).

Cross-entropy classification and CORN ordinal regression trade accuracy against ordinal concordance, whereas the pairwise ranking methods give the best ordinal metrics. SpineRankNet reaches the highest agreement (QWK 0.76), the lowest MAE (0.14) and the highest MCC (0.59) while keeping classification-level discrimination (ROC-AUC 0.94) and a balanced accuracy close to CE (63.3% vs. 64.1%), i.e. it reduces large grade errors without giving up accuracy, and it outperforms the prior ranking models RankNet and DeepRankSVM.
